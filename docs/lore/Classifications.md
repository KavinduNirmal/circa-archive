# Incident classifications

An [Incident](Anomalies.md) may be harmless, difficult to survive, or capable of ending the society from which its explorers departed.

A classification summarizes an assessment of those consequences. Read it with the account that supports it.

## The scale

| Class | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **E** | Established as non-hostile or harmless under known conditions. Size, reach, and influence do not by themselves exclude an Incident from this class. |
| **D** | Low risk. Deaths are uncommon and survival procedures are relatively reliable. |
| **C** | Moderate risk. Lethal consequences are established and potentially manageable. |
| **B** | High risk. Significant fatalities or unreliable survival procedures. |
| **A** | Extreme risk. Survival is highly uncertain, with substantial casualty potential. |
| **S** | Catastrophic. More than 1,000 confirmed deaths, or comparable disaster potential. |
| **O — Omega** | A credible extinction-level threat. |
| **U** | Unclassified. Information is insufficient or contradictory. |

Death counts are important evidence. They are not the only evidence relevant to an explorer's chances of returning or ending a manifestation.

## Class E

An E assessment concerns established behavior under known conditions. It is not a guarantee about conditions that have not been encountered.

A phenomenon need not be small to be harmless. A separate space may provide something useful without endangering the people who enter it. A small object may do the same without admitting anyone at all.

A room in which a fox offers counselling and a radio that describes the listener's daily fortune as a weather forecast illustrate this distinction. Their unusual nature does not, by itself, make them dangerous.

Usefulness alone is not sufficient for E. An account of a useful effect does not establish harmlessness or non-hostility. Retain the conditions supporting the assessment.

## Grounds for an assessment

Keep recorded deaths separate from injuries, missing people, and deaths said to be required by a resolution. A demand has not necessarily been carried out. A disappearance has not necessarily been explained.

Where difficulty affects the class, describe the difficulty. Surviving the encounter, completing an objective, clearing a manifestation, and resetting it are different outcomes. An escape route is not evidence that every other objective can be achieved by following it.

A classification without its supporting account leaves the next explorer unable to judge which of those questions was answered.

## Changing assessments

A low historical death toll can coexist with an extremely difficult clearing condition. [Intelligent or adaptive phenomena](Phenomena.md) may alter their behavior between investigations, including after repeated resets.

Such change remains bounded by the [operative rules](Rules.md#intelligence-and-its-limits). Higher-classification exceptions do not make every rule negotiable or give an actor unrestricted power to defeat a lawful loophole.

The phenomenon may change before its filed rank does. Read the class with the observations and conditions that supported it. A previously reliable procedure may no longer support the same assessment.

## Exploration guidance

Most Incidents ranked above C are not recommended for solo exploration. This warning applies to B, A, S, and Omega assessments. It does not establish that every Incident at C or below is suitable for a lone explorer.

U supplies no assurance of safety.

## The cost of resolution

An Incident may appear to require deaths or sacrifices before it can be cleared. The explorer must exercise discretion in weighing that cost against the survival of society.

Record the basis for the claimed requirement, the consequences expected if it is not met, and the decision made. Preserve the distinction between what the phenomenon demanded, what the explorer believed necessary, and what actually occurred.

CIRCA can preserve that decision. Filing it does not absolve the person who made it.
